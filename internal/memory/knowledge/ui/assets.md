# Choosing and making visual assets

## Choice
Choose an asset because it communicates a specific thing: the product's output, a location, a material, a process, an emotion or a fictional world. Write its role before selecting its style. A diagram can explain a relationship better than a stock photo; an expressive illustration can establish a world better than another generic geometric ornament.

Consider alternatives with their tradeoffs. Real product captures provide concrete evidence but require a working state and honest provenance. Photography provides material detail and human context but needs appropriate rights, consistent art direction and careful cropping. Vector illustration gives scalable geometry and precise alignment. Procedural shapes or canvas rendering suit repeated states and responsive visuals. Typography alone can be stronger when the content is the subject. Generated raster assets can supply distinctive imagery, but require inspection, consistent direction and honest labelling where provenance matters.

## Asset brief
Specify the subject, composition, viewing distance, palette, lighting or line treatment, background behavior, and required variants. For an icon set, specify stroke weight, optical size, corner character and fill conventions. Use familiar symbols for familiar actions and labels for ambiguous meanings. Emoji glyphs vary across platforms and rarely form a controlled icon system.

Create a small asset matrix: role, source or construction method, required dimensions, transparent/opaque background, alternate text purpose, licensing/provenance status, and fallback. Do not invent a library URL or claim a license without checking it. Prefer a verified local asset or a simple original vector when external availability is uncertain. Do not present synthetic testimonials, customers, screenshots or metrics as real evidence.

## Integration
Judge assets in the actual layout, not in isolation. Check crop behavior at narrow widths, text overlap, contrast, scaling, pixelation and loading failures. A transparent image can disappear over an unexpected surface; a photograph may lose its subject under a responsive crop. Reserve space to avoid layout jumps and provide a meaningful fallback.

Use one or two distinctive motifs with discipline. Repeating sparkles, orbs, blurred gradients, dotted grids and unrelated 3D objects across every section adds visual noise without a concept. If a decorative asset is removable without changing meaning, atmosphere or hierarchy, consider removing it. If it carries the product's character, give it enough quality and space to earn that role.
